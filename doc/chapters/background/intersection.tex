\section{Intersection Theory}

An important tool in algebraic geometry is intersection theory. While we will only use it explicitly in a few cases in this thesis, it is a foundational concept which is useful for building intuition. We will follow the treatment of intersection theory of divisors as presented in the first chapter of \cite{BEA96}.

Let $X$ be a smooth variety.  Recall that the Picard group $\mathrm{Pic}(X)$ is the group of isomorphism classes of line bundles $\mathcal{L}$ (i.e. of invertible sheaves) on $X$, with addition given by the tensor product, $[\mathcal{L}] + [\mathcal{L'}]$ = $[\mathcal{L} \otimes \mathcal{L'}]$. With every divisor $D$ on $X$, we associate a line bundle $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$, defined locally as the set of rational functions $f$ on $X$ such that $\text{Div}(f) + D \geq 0$. If $D$ is effective, $\mathcal{O}_X(D)$ comes with a canonical section $s \in H^0(X, \mathcal{O}_X(D))$, $s \neq 0$ which is unique up to scalar multiplication, such that $\mathrm{Div}(s) = D$. The map $D \mapsto \mathcal{O}_X(D)$ identifies $\mathrm{Pic}(X)$ with the group of linear equivalence classes of divisors on $X$. 


Let $Y$ be another smooth variety, and consider a morphism $f\colon X \to Y$. Then we can define the pullback of an invertible sheaf with respect to $f$ (\cite[Section 16.5]{EO25}) to get a homomorphism $f^* \colon \mathrm{Pic}(Y) \to \mathrm{Pic}(X)$. If $f$ is a dominant morphism, we can also define the pullback $f^*D$ of a Cartier divisor $D$ on $Y$ as a Cartier divisor on $X$ such that $f^*(\mathcal{O}_Y(D)) = \mathcal{O}_X(f^*D)$. See \cite[Section 20.7]{EO25} for details

We now define intersection multiplicity.

\begin{definition}
    Let $C, C'$ be two distinct irreducible curves on a surface $S$, $x \in C \cap C'$, $\mathcal{O}_x$ the local ring of $S$ at $x$. If $f$ and $g$ are local equations of $C$ and $C'$ in $\mathcal{O}_x$, then the \emph{intersection multiplicity} of $C$ and $C'$ at $x$ is defined as
    \[m_x(C\cap C') = \dim_\mathbb{C} \mathcal{O}_x/(f,g).\]
\end{definition}


\begin{definition}
    If $C, C'$ are two distinct irreducible curves on a surface $S$, then the \emph{intersection number} of $C$ and $C'$ is defined as
    \[C.C' = \sum_{x \in C \cap C'} m_x(C \cap C').\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
    Let $C, C'$ be two distinct irreducible curves on a surface $S$. Then 
    \[\mathcal{O}_S(C).\mathcal{O}_S(C') = C.C'.\]
\end{theorem}

In other words, we can compute the intersection number of two curves by computing the intersection number of their associated line bundles. If $D, D'$ are two divisors on $S$ we write $D.D'$ for $\mathcal{O}_S(D).\mathcal{O}_S(D')$. Then we have that we can calculate this intersection number by replacing $D$ or $D'$ by linearly equivalent divisors. This allows us to for example compute the self-intersection number of a divisor, denoted $D.D = D^2$. Next we give some applications of this, which also serve as instructive examples of working with intersection theory.

\begin{example}[Example I.9 (a) in \cite{BEA96}]
    Let $S = \mathbb{P}^2$. We know that $\mathrm{Pic}(S) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. Let $C, C'$ be curves of degree $d$ and $d'$ respectively. Since $C \equiv dL$ and $C' \equiv d'L'$ for distinct lines $L, L'$, we get Bezout's theorem:
    \[C.C' = dL.d'L' = dd' (L.L) = dd'.\]

\end{example}

\begin{example}[Proposition I.8 (a) in \cite{BEA96}]
    Let $C$ be a smooth curve, and let $f\colon S \to C$ be a surjective morphism from a smooth and projective surface $S$ to $C$, with $F$ a fiber of $f$. Then $F^2 = 0$.
\end{example}
\begin{proof}
    $F = f^*[x]$ for some point $x \in C$. Then there exists a divisor $A$ on $C$ linearly equivalent to $x$ (all points are linearly equivalent on a curve), such that $x \notin A$. Then $F \equiv f^*A$. Since $f^*A$ is a linear combination of fibers of $f$ all distinct from $F$, we have $F^2 = F.f^*A = 0$.
\end{proof}

Now we consider the case of a blow-up. Let $S$ be a smooth surface, and let $\pi\colon \hat{S} \to S$ be the blow-up of $S$ at a point $p \in S$. Let $E = \pi^{-1}(p)$ be the exceptional divisor, which we recall is isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1$. Also recall that for any curve $C$ on $S$, we can consider the strict transform $\hat{C}$ of $C$ on $\hat{S}$, defined as the closure of $\pi^{-1}(C \setminus \{p\})$ in $\hat{S}$. Then we have the following lemma on the pullback of divisors under the blow-up map.
\begin{lemma}[Lemma II.2 in \cite{BEA96}]\label{lem:strict}
    Let $C$ be an irreducible curve on $S$ which passes through $p$ with multiplicity $m$. Then $\pi^*(C) = \hat{C} + mE$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}[Proposition II.3 (b) in \cite{BEA96}]
    Let $S$ be a surface, $\pi\colon \hat{S} \to S$ blow up of a point $p \in X$ and $E \subseteq \hat{S}$ the exceptional curve. Let $D, D'$ be two divisors on $S$. Then $(\pi^*D).(\pi^*D') = D.D'$, $E.(\pi^*D) = 0$, $E^2 = -1$
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
    For the first two equalities, we can replace $D$ and $D'$ by linearly equivalent divisors not passing through $p$, so that their strict transforms do not intersect $E$, and their intersection numbers can be computed on $S$. For the last equality, choose a curve $C$ passing through $p$ with multiplicity 1. So its strict transform intersects $E$ transversely at one point, and $\hat{C}.E = 1$. Since $\hat{C} = \pi^*C - E$ by \cref{lem:strict}, and so $(\pi^*C - E).E = 1$. We know from the second equality that $\pi^*C.E = 0$, so $-E^2 = 1$. Then we get $E^2 = -1$.
\end{proof}


Recall that a divisor $D$ on a variety $S$ is \emph{ample} if some positive multiple of $D$ defines an embedding of $S$ into projective space. We now state the Hodge Index Theorem for surfaces.
\begin{theorem}[Hodge Index Theorem]\label{thm:hodge}
    Let $H$ be an ample divisor on a smooth surface $S$, and suppose $D$ is a divisor, $D \neq 0$, with $D.H = 0$. Then $D^2 < 0$.
\end{theorem}
The proof can be found in \cite{Ha77} Theorem V.1.9.
